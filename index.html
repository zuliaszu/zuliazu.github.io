About

I'm the Security Leader for the AWS Generative AI Innovation Center in EMEA. I review AI systems for security risks and work with delivery teams to address them.

My route here started in software development. I taught algorithms and data structures at Durham University, where I studied Computer Science, before joining AWS in DevOps. I later moved into security, risk and compliance, and now focus on AI security.

Much of my work is about protecting data and controlling what AI systems can access. That includes data residency and retention, agent permissions, and the evidence teams need for security and compliance reviews.

I also help run AWS Cloud Women Manchester and mentor women moving into cloud and security. Teaching has been part of my work since university. This guide is another way to share what I've learned.

Positions

How I think about security

Positions I keep coming back to, taken from things I have written and talked about.

  1. 01

    AI security overlaps, not replaces

    I see AI security and cybersecurity as overlapping fields, like a Venn diagram, not one subsuming the other. AI's probabilistic nature and growing autonomy still deserve tailored defences even where existing controls help.

  2. 02

    Data attacks start early

    The biggest risks to data are poisoning training data and manipulating features or datasets, and they show up at the earliest stages of an attack, not at the model. If training data is not validated, everything downstream inherits the problem.

  3. 03

    Supply chain is the whole pipeline

    A model file, a third-party library, a CI/CD pipeline, a cloud provider: any one of these can be the entry point, so I treat supply chain risk as running through the entire life cycle, not just the dependencies list.

  4. 04

    Structure follows headcount, not fashion

    Structure should follow headcount and risk profile. Work out which parts of the CIA triad your threat profile actually stresses, staff for that, and choose an org model deliberately rather than inheriting one.

  5. 05

    FDEs are an access question

    Forward deployed engineers exist because the bottleneck moved from model capability to deployment reality. For anyone in security this matters twice: FDEs get deep access to production systems by design, and what they leave behind becomes part of the attack surface someone has to defend.

The reason is blunt: the hard part of AI is not the model.
