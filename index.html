About

I am the Security Leader for the Generative AI Innovation Center in EMEA at AWS. I am responsible for the secure delivery of client engagements: I review what 40+ scientists and 15+ strategists build, identify and mitigate risk in AI-enabled systems, and research novel attack methods against AI and ML. I also present to customers and at conferences on securing AI systems.

I have eight years in engineering, DevOps and security, the last five in security. I have run more than 45 engagement security assessments for banks, insurers, automotive finance, pharma, energy and public sector organisations, across the UK, EU, Middle East and Africa.

My specialisms are EU data residency and cross-region inference boundaries, data retention and Zero Data Retention, GDPR and DPIA-aligned data classification, EU AI Act risk triage, and agentic AI security, including MCP and autonomous agents. Day to day that means Bedrock Guardrails, IAM and Zero Trust, EKS, SIEM and SOAR, and audits against ISO 27001, NIST CSF and PCI DSS. I wrote a GenAI security capture-the-flag on prompt injection chains, guardrail bypass and data exfiltration through tool use, which was selected for AWS re:Invent 2026 GameDay.

The plan on this site exists because security people kept asking me the same question. It is for people who already have security fundamentals and want to know which AI security role fits them, what to learn, and in what order.

Before security I built a manufacturing scheduling optimisation at Nissan Trading Corporation, and taught algorithms and data structures at Durham University, where I read Computer Science. I lead AWS Cloud Women Manchester, the largest AWS Cloud Women chapter in the UK, and mentor women moving into cloud and security. I have been featured in BBC News coverage of women in computing, and I won the Professor Sue Black Award for Technology Evangelism.

Positions

How I think about security

Positions I keep coming back to, taken from things I have written and talked about.

  1. 01

    AI security overlaps, not replaces

    I see AI security and cybersecurity as overlapping fields, like a Venn diagram, not one subsuming the other. AI's probabilistic nature and growing autonomy still deserve tailored defences even where existing controls help.

  2. 02

    Data attacks start early

    The biggest risks to data are poisoning training data and manipulating features or datasets, and they show up at the earliest stages of an attack, not at the model. If training data is not validated, everything downstream inherits the problem.

  3. 03

    Supply chain is the whole pipeline

    A model file, a third-party library, a CI/CD pipeline, a cloud provider: any one of these can be the entry point, so I treat supply chain risk as running through the entire life cycle, not just the dependencies list.

  4. 04

    Structure follows headcount, not fashion

    Structure should follow headcount and risk profile. Work out which parts of the CIA triad your threat profile actually stresses, staff for that, and choose an org model deliberately rather than inheriting one.

  5. 05

    FDEs are an access question

    Forward deployed engineers exist because the bottleneck moved from model capability to deployment reality. For anyone in security this matters twice: FDEs get deep access to production systems by design, and what they leave behind becomes part of the attack surface someone has to defend.

The reason is blunt: the hard part of AI is not the model.
