Prove it: Reproduce a published attack result

About 10 hours, alone, free tools. Certificates say you sat a course; this says you can do the job.

Pick one published paper describing an attack or defence against language models and reproduce its core result on a small open model you run yourself. Turning a claim in a paper into working code and an honest result is the daily work of this role, and it is what a certificate cannot show.

  1. Pick one paper with a concrete, testable claim, for example a jailbreak technique, a prompt injection method, or a defence that claims to block one of those.
  2. Install a small open model you can run locally or cheaply, for example through Ollama, or with Hugging Face transformers on a free-tier GPU notebook such as Google Colab or Kaggle.
  3. Read the paper's method section twice and write down, in your own words, the exact steps needed to reproduce the result before writing any code.
  4. Write a script that runs the attack or defence against your model and records inputs, outputs, and whether the result matches the paper's claim.
  5. Run the experiment at least 10 times with varied inputs, not just the paper's exact examples, and log every failure as well as every success.
  6. Compare your results to the paper's reported numbers and write down where you agree, disagree, or could not tell.
  7. Write up the work as a short report: what you tried, what worked, what did not, and why your setup differs from the paper's.
  8. Publish the write-up somewhere checkable, for example a personal blog post or a public repo with the log included.

Deliverable: A short written report plus the runnable code and logs, showing a real attempt to reproduce someone else's published security result on a model you controlled.

Self-check

  • the code runs end to end without manual fixing when someone else tries it.
  • the write-up states clearly whether the result was reproduced, partially reproduced, or not reproduced.
  • at least one failed run or unexpected result is documented, not hidden.
  • a reader with no context could follow the steps and get a similar result.
  • the report names the model, versions, and exact prompts or inputs used.
